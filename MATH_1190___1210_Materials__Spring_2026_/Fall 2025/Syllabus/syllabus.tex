	\section{Introduction}

	    Have you ever thought about how to describe change? When driving, the car would have a speed, which represents how fast its position changes. When a population grows, however often we measure it, daily, monthly, or annually, it would have a rate of change. Our course is all about how we work with change from a mathematical perspective. And whose life doesn't have changes in them? That's why this course is relevant to you.

        Have you ever worked with uncertainty? What are the chances that it'll rain today? How likely is it for me to win this raffle? How would the election turn out? Uncertainty is present in our day-to-day life. Although we won't ever be able to predict future, we can describe and interpret uncertainty using mathemtics.
        
        This course will introduce a mathematical toolkit for understanding change and uncertainty. Understanding change and uncertainty helps us understand the world:

        \begin{itemize}
            %\item A biologist might be interested in population variation over time.
            \item A medical researcher might be interested in a model of blood pressure as a function of body weight, or concentration of a drug in the bloodstream over time.
            \item A business executive might study the demand for a product as a function of its price or, perhaps, as it relates to the amount spent on marketing.
            \item An environmental scientist might be interested in modeling the level of a toxin in a lake over time.
            \item A data scientist might be interested in fitting a regression line to a data set to make predictions.
        \end{itemize}

        In this course, we will study the properties and applications of two fundamental tools for understanding change and uncertainty: the derivative, which represents the rate of change of a function, and the definite integral, which can be used to compute net change. %Both derivatives and definite integrals are built using the notion of limits, which constitutes the idea of getting closer and closer to something. 

        In terms of our course structure, one big thing is that our course is a coordinated course. This means that all sections, regardless of who the instructor is, cover the same material and take the same exams. We also share the same Canvas site across different sections, so you are able to check out other sections' material if you would like! %We will be using \href{https://openstax.org/details/books/calculus-volume-1}{\it Openstax Calculus 1} as our optional textbook.

        \section{Assessments for Learning}

        A variety of assessments are designed for our course with one common purpose: to support and assess your learning. Therefore, it is important to dedicate yourself to working on these assessments in your day-to-day learning. 
        
        \subsection{Pre-Class Assignments}
        
        The learning of each of our lessons starts with a pre-class assignment. The pre-class assignments are designed to support your initial encounter of the course material. The format of the pre-classments could vary from lesson to lesson, but often times it involves watching some short videos and answering some related questions. \textbf{The pre-class assignments are due by the start time of the corresponding lesson with no extensions.} There are unlimited attempts for each pre-class assignment, with the highest attempt counting toward your grade.
        
        If you come up with any questions about the content while working on the pre-class assignment, please bring the questions to class, so that your instructor can answer it!
        
        Your three lowest pre-class assignment grades will be dropped automatically.
        
        \subsection{Activities}
        
        Throughout the semester, your instructor might conduct a variety of activities, such as the "How to Study'' modules on Canvas, the Application Days during class, or occasional self-reflections, to support your learning. Some of the learning activities occur in-class while others occur outside our classroom. 

        Math is social. A lot of folks learn math through talking about problems with others. That's why we are primarily spending the class time for you to practice on your skills. For each lesson, you learn the fundamental concepts in the pre-class assignment, so that you are ready to ask questions and attempt problems with the guidance of your instructor and help of your peers. Your instructor might run many different learning activities during class. Please be prepared to be present and ready to participate - that's how you learn!

        Outside of the classroom, learning math can be an individual or a group experience based on your choice. In either case, we have prepared some self-reflection assignments for your to look back at your learning process, track your personal growth over time, and learn from the mistakes you've made (if any). These reflection assignments will be posted to Canvas with at least a 5-day notice. In addition, we have prepared some learning modules on how to study for you to learn from outside of class, which are also available through Canvas.

        \subsection{Growth-Based Grading}

        The growth-based grading scheme is designed for students to learn and grow over time. In this scheme, learning is assessed through 4 semester tests containing questions pertaining to specific mathematical skills – called learning targets – with one numbered question per learning target. Scoring and feedback on each learning target is given not using points, but a proficiency scale that helps learners understand their progress.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Exceeds Expectations}: All important ideas are present in the response with no mistakes.
            \item \textbf{Proficient}: All important ideas are present in the response, but with a limited number of minor mistakes (such as arithmetic mistakes, miscopying work from a previous line, etc.) %to moderate mistakes (such as an algebra or calculus mistake not preventing demonstration of important ideas).
            \item \textbf{Almost}: Most important ideas are present in the response.
            \item \textbf{Developing}: Some important ideas are present in the response.
            \item \textbf{Emerging}: Few important ideas are present in the response.
            \item \textbf{Not Yet}: No important ideas are yet present in the response
        \end{itemize}

        The benefit of the growth-based scheme is, students will get to attempt each learning target multiple times to demonstrate proficiency! For each learning target, your highest attempt will count toward your final grade. In particular, for learning targets appearing on Knowledge Checkpoints 1, 2, and 3, you will have the opportunity to re-attempt them during the immediately preceding Knowledge Checkpoint and during/after the final. Therefore, you have 3 attempts for most learning targets appearing in Knowledge Checkpoints 1-4, and your highest score out of the three attempts will be kept. For more details of which learning target is appearing when, please visit our \textbf{\href{https://canvas.its.virginia.edu/courses/177534/pages/knowledge-checkpoints?module_item_id=1939837}} page on Canvas.

        %For example, F1 is a target that is first appearing on Knowledge Checkpoint 1. So you can attempt it during Knowledge Checkpoint 1, Knowledge Checkpoint 2, and immediately after the final.

        The only targets with one attempt are the 10 FE learning targets on the final. They are counted separately from the core targets during the semester, and they are considered non-core. You only have one attempt on them, which is during the final. Nevertheless, if you perform better on these targets during the final than during the semester, the better scores will be used toward the corresponding learning targets as well.

    \section{Honor Policy}

    We trust every student our course to fully comply with all of the provisions of the University’s Honor Code. By enrolling in this course, you have agreed to abide by and uphold the Honor System of the University of Virginia.

    The Honor Code is breached when a student submits work that does not represent the capabilities of that student, within the instructions of the assessment. When an instructor suspects academic dishonesty has occurred, they will discuss the incident with the other instructors. The instructor may also reach out to associated student(s) for more information. If the instructors collectively agree that academic dishonesty has occurred, then a score of 0 will be assigned on the associated graded item. Additionally, all suspected violations will be forwarded to the Honor Committee, Work on assessments should represent what a student – and only that student – was able to do during that assessment. 
    
    For example, the following are considered academic dishonesty:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Possess and/or use calculators, dictionaries, notes, AI, or any other kind of aid (that is, anything that could reasonably be used to obtain information) during a Knowledge Checkpoint or the final exam.
        \item Look at another student’s paper during or while submitting a Knowledge Checkpoint or the final exam.
        \item Communicate in any way with anyone else during or while submitting a Knowledge Checkpoint or the final exam.
        \item Leave the classroom to look up information on your phone during a Knowledge Checkpoint or the final exam.
        \item Attempt to take a make-up test outside the allowed window.
        \item Copy another student's work for any homework assignment.
    \end{itemize}

    The above list is not intended to be exhaustive. The basic question at hand here is whether or not the work provided is truly the work of the student who submitted it. When that is not the case, then academic dishonesty has occurred.
    
    Working with a professor, tutor, or friend to clarify your ideas and organization for homework assignments is generally not plagiarism. In addition, you are welcome to use a calculator while doing in-class work or homework. 
    
    If you have any questions about regarding the course Honor policy, please consult with your instructor. There is no penalty for honest inquiry or confusion! If you believe you may have committed an Honor Offense, you may wish to file a Conscientious Retraction by calling the Honor Offices at (434) 924-7602. For your retraction to be considered valid, it must, among other things, be filed with the Honor Committee before you are aware that the act in question has come under suspicion by anyone. More information can be found \href{http://www.virginia.edu/honor}{here}.
