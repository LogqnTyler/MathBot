\item Consider the following function $f(x)$.

\[f(x)=x^2 - x\]%-(x-1)^4(15x^2+18x+7)\]

%\[f'(x)=15x^2(x-1)(x+1)\]%10(1-x)^3(3x+1)^2\]

You are required to use methods we've learned in our course, such as the first derivative test, the second derivative test, the close interval method, or the first derivative test for absolute extrema, to solve this problem. In addition, you need to make sure the method you use is suitable for the question.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Find all critical numbers of $f(x)$.
\smallskip

{\color{blue}
The derivative is \( f'(x) = 2x-1 \). Since this is a polynomial function, there are no solutions to \( f'(x) \) DNE. The solution to \( f'(x) = 0 \) is \( x = \frac{1}{2} \). This is in the domain of \( f \), and so the only critical number of \( f \) is \( x = \frac{1}{2} \).

}
\bigskip    

    \item For each critical number found in part (a), classify it as a local minimum, a local maximum, or neither. Fully justify your work.
\smallskip

{\color{blue}
The domain of \( f \) is all real numbers, and this is split into the two intervals \( (-\infty, \frac{1}{2}) \) and \( (\frac{1}{2}, \infty) \) by the critical number \( x = \frac{1}{2} \). The sign of the derivative on these intervals is:
\begin{table}[h!]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
      $x$  & $2x-1$ & $f'(x)$\\
      \hline
        $(-\infty, \frac{1}{2})$ & -  & - \\
        $(\frac{1}{2}, \infty)$ & +  &  + \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

As the derivative changes from negative to positive at the critical number $x=\frac{1}{2}$, the First Derivative Test implies that $f$ has a local minimum at $x=\frac{1}{2}$.
}
\bigskip    

    \item Find the $x$-values of all absolute extrema of $f(x)$ on the interval $[-2, 2]$.
\smallskip

{\color{blue}
We use the closed interval method, as $[-2,2]$ is indeed a closed interval. We evaluate:
\[
f(-2) = 6, \quad f(2) = 2, \quad f\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{-1}{4},
\]
where we also use from (a) that the only critical number of $f$ is $x=\frac{1}{2}$. So, on $[-2,2]$, $f(x)$ has absolute maximum at $x=-2$ and absolute minimum at $x=\frac{1}{2}$.
\medskip

The conclusion that \(x=\frac12\) is the absolute minimum also follows from the First Derivative Test for absolute minima, since by (a) there is only one critical number where the derivative \(f'\) changes sign from negative to positive.
}
\end{enumerate}

To receive full credit, make sure to clearly state:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The function you wish to maximize\\
    \item A domain/interval for optimization\\
    \item The dimensions that maximize the area\\
    \item The maximium area\\
    \item A mathematical justification that this is the maximum area.
\end{itemize}
{\color{blue}
\begin{itemize}
\item
\textbf{Use the restriction} on fencing:
$2y+x=100\ \text{ft}\ \Longrightarrow\ x=100-2y\
\left(\text{or}\ y=\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot(100-x)\right)$.
\item
Write the \textbf{function you wish to optimize}.
In our case it should be area:\\
$A(y) = xy = (100-2y)y\
\left(\text{or}\
A(x) = xy = \frac{x}{2} (100-x)
\right)$.
\item
The \textbf{domain} of $A(y)$
(or $A(x)$) is $[0,\infty)$.
\item
Find \textbf{the critical numbers} of $A(y)$.
Since $A'(y)=100-4y$ is defined everywhere, the only critical numbers is the $y$-value where $A'(y)=0$
which is $y=25$.\\
(Similarly, if we expressed $x$ instead of $y$,
$A'(x)=50-x\ \Longrightarrow$\
the only critical number is $x=50$)
\item
Use the \textbf{sign line} to determine \textbf{local extrema}:
\begin{table}[h!]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
      $y$  & $A'(y)=100-4y$ \\
      \hline
        $(0,25)$ & +  \\
        $(25,\infty)$ & - \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}\\
Hence, $y=25$ is a local maximum for $A(y)$.
(Similarly, can justify that $x=50$
is a local maximum of $A(x)$).
\item
Since $y=25$ is \textbf{the only critical number} of $A(y)$ on $[0,\infty)$,
by \textbf{the $1^\text{st}$ Derivative Test for Absolute Extrema},
it is an absolute maximum of $A(y)$.
(Similarly for $x=50$)
\item
\textbf{Answer:} $x=50$\ ft and $y=25$\ ft
maximize the area.
The maximum area is $50\cdot 25=1250$\ ft.
\end{itemize}}

\item Suppose the following values of definite integrals are given.

\[
\int_{0}^{3} g(x)\,dx = 5
\quad
\int_{-2}^{0} g(x)\,dx = -2
\quad

\tasksA{2}{

\task $\displaystyle \int_{0}^{3} f(x)\, dx$\\

\(\begin{aligned}
\text{Since }\int_{3}^{0} 2(f(x) - g(x))\,dx &= 2\int_{3}^{0} f(x) \ dx  -  2\int_{3}^{0}g(x)\,dx\\ \text{ (by linearity )}\\
&=10 \text{ and }\\
\int_{0}^{3}g(x)\,dx &= 5,
\end{aligned}\)
by the reverse limits property, 
\begin{align*}
10 &= 2\int_{3}^{0} f(x) \ dx  - 2 \cdot (-5)  \\
&= 2\int_{3}^{0} f(x) \ dx  + 10 \text{ so }\\
0 &= \int_{3}^{0} f(x) \, dx \text{ and }\\
\int_{0}^{3} f(x) \, dx &= - \int_{3}^{0} f(x) \, dx \\
&= -0\\
&= 0
\end{align*}
}

\item For each of the following definite integrals, determine whether the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus can be applied to evaluate. 

If so, evaluate the integral using the FTC. There's no need to simplify your final answer.

If not, clearly explain why.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2}\dfrac{1}{x-1}\, dx$\\

    {\color{blue} The fundamental theorem of calculus does not apply to this integral because the integrand $f(x) = \frac{1}{x - 1}$ is not continuous on the domain of integration. There is an infinite discontinuity at $x = 1$ and $0 < 1 < 2$.}
\vfill
    \item $\displaystyle\int_{0}^1  \frac{x^2}{2}+ \frac{5}{6}\, dx$
{\color{blue}
\begin{align*}
     \displaystyle\int_{0}^1  \frac{x^2}{2}+ \frac{5}{6}\, dx &= \left(\frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{5}{6}x \right)\bigg|_{0}^1\\
     &= \left(\frac{(1)^3}{6} + \frac{5}{6}(1)\right) - \left(\frac{(0)^3}{6} + \frac{5}{6}(0)\right)\\
     &= \frac{1}{6} + \frac{5}{6} - 0\\
     &= \frac{6}{6}\\
     &= 1
\end{align*}

}
    \vfill

%    \item $\displaystyle\int_{-3}^1\dfrac{1}{x^2}- \frac{x^2}{2}\, dx$

%    \vfill
   \item $\displaystyle\int_{1}^2\dfrac{1}{x}- \frac{x}{2}\, dx$
   {\color{blue}
   \begin{align*}
       \displaystyle\int_{1}^2\dfrac{1}{x}- \frac{x}{2}\, dx &= \left(\ln|x| - \frac{x^2}{4}\right)\bigg|_{1}^2\\

       &= \ln(2) - 1 + \frac14\\
       &= \ln(2) - \frac{3}{4}
   \end{align*}
   
   }

    \vfill
\end{enumerate}

Variance measures how spread out a distribution is around its mean. So we compare how concentrated or dispersed each histogram is.

\medskip

\textbf{$X_2$.} \\
The distribution of $X_2$ is roughly uniform across all values from $0$ to $5$. This means the probability is evenly spread out, so values are more dispersed from the mean compared to $X_1$. Thus, $X_2$ has a \textbf{larger variance than $X_1$}, but not the largest overall.

\medskip

\textbf{$X_3$.} \\
The distribution of $X_3$ places most of its probability mass at the extreme values (near $0$ and $5$), with very little probability in the middle. This means observations are typically far from the mean, leading to large deviations. Therefore, $X_3$ has the \textbf{largest variance}.

\medskip

\textbf{Conclusion:} \\
Ranking from smallest to largest variance:
\[
X_1 \;<\; X_2 \;<\; X_3.
\]

\item The lifetime, $X$, in tens of hours, of a battery has (potential) probability density function $p(x)$ given by
\[
p(x) =
\begin{cases}
0, & x < 1, \\
c(x - 1), & 1 \le x \le 3, \\
0, & x > 3.
\end{cases}
\]
\bigskip

\begin{enumerate}[i]
\item Find the value of $c$ that will make $p(x)$ into a valid probability density function.

\vspace{0.3cm}
{\tiny
\textcolor{blue}{
We require
\[
\int_{1}^{3} c(x-1)\,dx = 1.
\]
Compute:
\(\begin{aligned}
c\int_{1}^{3} (x-1)\,dx &= c\left[\frac{x^2}{2} - x\right]_{1}^{3}\\
&= c\left(\frac{(3)^2}{2}  - 3\right) -  c\left(\frac{(1)^2}{2}  - 1\right)\\
&= c\left(\frac{9}{2} - \frac{6}{2}\right) - c\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{2}\right)\\
&= c\frac{3}{2} - c\left(- \frac{1}{2} \right)\\
& = c\frac{3}{2} + c\frac{1}{2}\\
& = c\frac{4}{2}\\
& = c\cdot 2\\
\end{aligned}\)\\
So
\(
c \cdot 2 = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad c = \frac{1}{2}.
\)
}
}
\vspace{0.8cm}

\item What is the support of $X$?

\bigskip

$\mathrm{Supp}_{X}$ = \underline{\textcolor{blue}{[1,3]}}

\bigskip

\item Find $P(2 \le X \le 3)$.

% \vspace{0.3cm}
{\tiny
\textcolor{blue}{
\(\begin{aligned}
P(2 \le X \le 3) &= \int_{2}^{3} \frac{1}{2}(x-1)\,dx\\
&= \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{x^2}{2} - x\right]_{2}^{3}\\
&= \frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{9}{2} - 3\right) - \left(\frac{4}{2} - 2\right)\right)\\
&= \frac{1}{2}\cdot \left(\frac{3}{2} - 0\right)\\
&= \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{2}\\
&= \frac{3}{4}.
\end{aligned}\)
}
}
% \vspace{0.8cm}
\quad
\item Write down an expression for expectation $E[X]$ of $X$ (You do not need to perform any calculations).

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textcolor{blue}{
\[
E[X] = \int_{1}^{3} x \cdot \frac{1}{2}(x-1)\,dx.
\]
}
