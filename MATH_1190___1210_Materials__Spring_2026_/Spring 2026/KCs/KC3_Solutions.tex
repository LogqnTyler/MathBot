\item Find the derivative of the following expressions. 

Write down the steps so that you will be able to receive partial credit if your final answer is not fully correct. There's no need to simplify your final answer.

\begin{enumerate}
\item $f(x) = \dfrac{x \ln(x) + x}{e^x}$
\medskip

{\color{blue}
We have 
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} (x \ln(x) + x) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} (x \ln(x) ) + 1 = [x \cdot \frac{1}{x} + 1 \cdot \ln(x)] + 1 = 2 + \ln(x),
\]
using the sum rule, product rule, and derivatives of $x$ and $\ln(x)$. Using the derivative of $e^x$ and the quotient rule, we get
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}f(x) = \frac{e^x(2+\ln(x)) - (x \ln(x)+x)e^x}{(e^{x})^2}.
\]
}
\bigskip

\item $g(x) = \sqrt{e^{5x^2}-5}$
\medskip

{\color{blue}
Using the sum rule, and then the chain rule with inner function $5x^2$ and outer function $e^x$, we get
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(e^{5x^2}-5) = e^{5x^2}\cdot 10 x.
\]
Now using the chain rule with inner function $e^{5x^2}-5$ and outer function $\sqrt{x}$, we get
\[
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}g(x) = \frac12 (e^{5x^2}-5)^{-1/2} e^{5x^2} \cdot 10 x.
\]

}

\end{enumerate}

\item Let 
\[
f(x) = (x - 2)^{4/3}
\]

You may take as given that
\[
f'(x) = \frac{4}{3}(x - 2)^{1/3} , \quad \text{and} \quad f''(x) = \frac{4}{9(x-2)^{2/3}}.
\]

\begin{enumerate}

\item[(a)] Find all critical numbers of $f$.
\smallskip

{\color{blue}
To find critical numbers, we set $f'(x)$ to 0 or DNE. We see there are no possible values where $f'(x)$ is DNE, and $f'(x) = 0$ happens only for $x=2$. Since $x=2$ is also in the domain of the function $f(x)$, we conclude that $f$ has $x=2$ as the only critical number.
}
\medskip


\item[(b)] What does the First Derivative Test tell us about the behavior of $f$ at its critical numbers?  
You must show sign analysis to receive full credit.
\smallskip

{\color{blue}
The domain of $f(x)$ is all real numbers. The critical number $x=2$ splits this into the two intervals $(-\infty, 2)$ and $(2, \infty)$. We check the sign of the derivative on these separately:
\begin{table}[h!]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
      $x$  & $x-2$ & $f'(x)$\\
      \hline
        $(-\infty, 2)$ & -  & - \\
        $(2, \infty)$ & +  &  + \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

As the derivative changes from negative to positive at the critical number $x=2$, the First Derivative Test implies that $f$ has a local minimum at $x=2$.
}
\medskip

\item[(c)] What does the Second Derivative Test tell us about the behavior of $f$ at its critical numbers?  
\smallskip

{\color{blue}
We see that $f''(x)$ is not defined at the critical number $x=2$, and so the Second Derivative Test is inconclusive about the behaviour of $f$ at $x=2$.
}
\medskip

\item[(d)] On the interval $[0,3]$, determine where $f$ attains its absolute maximum and absolute minimum values. Clearly justify your answer.
\smallskip

{\color{blue}
We use the closed interval method, as $[0,3]$ is indeed a closed interval. We evaluate:
\[
f(0) = (-2)^{4/3} = 2 \sqrt[3]{2}, \quad f(3) = 1, \quad f(2) = 0,
\]
where we also use from (a) that the only critical number of $f$ is $x=2$. So, on $[0,3]$, $f(x)$ has absolute maximum at $x=0$ and absolute minimum at $x=2$.
}
\end{enumerate}

\item A Norman window has the shape of a rectangle with height $y$ surmounted by a semicircle with radius $x$ (see the accompanying figure). If a Norman window is to have a perimeter of (4 + $\pi$) ft, what should its dimensions be in order to allow the maximum area of light through the window?


\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{NormanWindow.png}
    \caption{Picture of a Norman Window}
    \label{fig:my_image}
\end{figure}

{\color{blue}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{1.}]
\textbf{Perimeter restriction.}
The perimeter of the window is computed as the sum of half circumference of
the top half circle and the perimeter of the rectangular part
\textbf{without the top side}:
\[
P = 2x + 2y + \pi x = (2+\pi) x  + 2y.
\]
The restriction on the perimeter
$P = (2+\pi) x  + 2y = (4 + \pi) \text{ ft}$
allows us express $y$ as
\[
y = \frac{1}{2}\left(4 + \pi - (2+\pi)x\right).
\]
\item[\textbf{2.}]
\textbf{Area function.}
The area of the window is computed as the sum of the area of the top
half circle and the area of the rectangular part:
\begin{align*}
A(x) &= \frac{\pi x^2}{2} + 2 x y
=
\frac{\pi x^2}{2} + 2 x\cdot \frac{1}{2}\left(4 + \pi - (2+\pi)x\right)
=
\frac{\pi x^2}{2} + 4x + \pi x - (2+\pi)x^2 \\
&= (4 + \pi)x - \left(2+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)x^2.
\end{align*}
Here we used the restriction on the perimeter (see part \textbf{1.})
in order to make $A$ a function of only one variable $x$.
Since $x$ is a length, the \textbf{domain}
of $A(x)$ is $[0,\infty)$.
\item[\textbf{3.}]
\textbf{Critical numbers.}
Compute the derivative of
$A(x)=(4 + \pi)x - \left(2+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)x^2$:
\[
A'(x) = (4 + \pi) - \left(4+\pi\right)x.
\]
Note that $A'(x)$ is defined everywhere and
$A'(x)=0$ only when $x=1$.
Since $x=1$ is in the domain of $A(x)$,
\textbf{$x=1$ is the only critical number of $A(x)$}.
\item[\textbf{4.}]
\textbf{Local and absolute extrema.}
Note that $A'(0) > 0$ and $A'(2) < 0$.
Thus, the critical number \textbf{$x=1$ is a local maximum of $A(x)$}.
Since $x=1$ is the only critical number
of $A(x)$ on its domain,
by
\textbf{the $1^\text{st}$ Derivative Test for
Absolute Extrema}
$A(1)=2+\frac{\pi}{2}$ is the absolute
maximum of $A(x)$.
\item[\textbf{5.}]
\textbf{Answer.}
The dimensions
$x=1$ ft and
$y=\frac{1}{2}\left(4 + \pi - (2+\pi)\cdot1\right)
=1$ ft
allow the maximum area of light through the window.
\end{enumerate}
}

\item Suppose $v(t)$ represents the velocity (in meters per second) of a particle moving along a straight line at time $t$ (in seconds), for $0 \leq t \leq 6$.

\begin{enumerate}

\item[(a)] What are the units of $\displaystyle \int_1^4 v(t)\,dt$?  

{\color{blue} The units are meters, since \[\left(\frac{\text{meters}}{\text{seconds}}\right)*(\text{seconds})=\text{meters}\]}

\item[(b)] Let $s(t)$ represent the position of the particle at time $t$, and assume that $s'(t) = v(t)$.  

Explain what the quantity
\[
\int_1^4 v(t)\,dt
\]
represents in terms of $s(t)$. Be sure to include all units.

{\color{blue} Given that $s'(t)=v(t)$, we can conclude that $\int_1^4v(t)\,dt=s(4)-s(1)$.\\ This quantity describes the difference in position (in meters) of the particle from time $t=1$ second to time $t=4$ seconds.}
\vspace{1cm}

\item[(c)] Suppose the particle reaches its absolute minimum position at $t = 2$.  
Determine which of the following integrals must be positive. Select all that apply.

\[
\text{(i)}\ \int_0^2 v(t)\,dt 
\qquad
\text{(ii)}\ \int_2^5 v(t)\,dt
\qquad
\text{(iii)}\ \int_0^5 v(t)\,dt
\qquad
\text{(iv)}\ \int_5^2 v(t)\,dt
\]
{\color{blue}The correct answer is (ii).\\ When written in terms of the position function $s(t)$, the quantities above are:
\[
\text{(i)}\ s(2)-s(0)
\qquad
\text{(ii)}\ s(5)-s(2)
\qquad
\text{(iii)}\ s(5)-s(0)
\qquad
\text{(iv)}\ s(2)-s(5)
\]
Given that the absolute minimum of $s(t)$ occurs when $t=2$, we can conclude that the quantities (i) and (iv) must be negative, and that the quantity (ii) must be positive. There is not enough information given to determine if the quantity (iii) is positive or negative.}

\vspace{2cm}

\item[(d)] Suppose $\displaystyle \int_0^6 v(t)\,dt = 0$.  
What does this tell you about the position of the particle at $t=6$ compared to its position at $t=0$?  
Explain your reasoning.

{\color{blue} This tells us that the particle is in the same position at $t=6$ and $t=0$. The particle returned to its original position.\\
In mathematical language, we know that $\int_0^6v(t)\,dt=s(6)-s(0)$. Then based on the assumption in the problem we conclude that $s(6)-s(0)=0$, or equivalently $s(6)=s(0)$. The position of the particle at time $t=6$ is equal to the position of the particle at time $t=0$.}

\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.25]
          \begin{axis}[
            xlabel={$t$},
            xtick={0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8},
            xticklabels={$0$,$1$,$2$,$3$,$4$,$5$,$6$,$7$},
            ytick={-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3},
            yticklabels={$-3$,$-2$,$-1$,$0$,$1$,$2$,$3$},
            samples=320,
            domain=-0.5:7.5,
            restrict y to domain=-3.5:3.5,

Use the properties of definite integrals (linearity, reversing limits, and interval addition) to answer the following questions. Show all work and clearly indicate where you have used any property of integration.

\begin{enumerate}

\item Find $\displaystyle \int_0^3 a(t)\,dt$.  

\vfill

\item Find $\displaystyle \int_3^5 a(t)\,dt$.

\item Suppose it is known that $\displaystyle \int_{3}^{-1} a(t)\,dt = 4$.

\end{enumerate}
