\section{Pedagogy}

\transition{Pedagogy}

\frame[t]{{Pedagogy}

{\color{red}}

How can we view students' mistakes?\\[0.1in]

\textbf{\underline{Slips, Errors, and Misconceptions}}

({\footnotesize adapted from {\color{blue}\href{http://academic.sun.ac.za/mathed/174/misconceptions.pdf}{Olivier}}, 1989})

\vfill

``{\bf Slips} are wrong answers due to {\it processing}. They are not systematic, but are spontaneously carelessly made by both experts and novices. They are easily detected and spontaneously corrected."

\vfill

``{\bf Errors} are wrong answers due to {\it planning}. They are systematic in that they [would be] applied regularly in the same circumstances."

\vfill

``Errors are the {\it symptoms} of the underlying conceptual structures that are the cause of errors. It is these underlying beliefs and principles in the cognitive structure that are the {\it cause} of systematic conceptual errors that I shall call {\bf misconceptions}."

\vfill

}

\frame[t]{{Pedagogy}


How can we view difficulty of a task from a students' perspective?\\[0.1in]

\textbf{\underline{Cognitive Demand}} 

({\footnotesize adapted from {\color{blue}\href{https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00028312033002455}{Stein et al}}, 1996})

\vfill

$\text{low-level demand }\begin{cases} \text{(1) Memorization tasks}\\
\text{(2) Procedural tasks}\end{cases}$

\vfill

$\text{high-level demand }\begin{cases} \text{(3) Connections tasks}\\
\text{(4) Mathematics tasks}\end{cases}$

\vfill

$\text{no demand }\begin{cases} \text{(0) student disengaged}\end{cases}$

\vfill
\textbf{Question:}  
Can you identify examples of low- vs high-level tasks in your own teaching? How might students perceive their difficulty?
}

\frame[t]{{Pedagogy}

How do these ideas inform our grading?

\begin{itemize}
\item Slips should {\it not} impact students' scores, and errors {\it should} impact students' scores when the errors are relevant to the learning target being tested.
\item Leniency should scale with cognitive demand. In particular, memorization/low cognitive demand tasks should be graded for correctness, and higher cognitive demand tasks should be graded for meaningful use of course concepts \& tools relevant to the learning target being tested.
\end{itemize}
}
%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Implications for Grading}
\textbf{How do these ideas inform our grading?}
\vspace{0.3cm}

\begin{itemize}
    \item When observing student work, how can you distinguish between a slip, an error, or a misconception?
    \item How might you adjust your grading rubrics for high-level tasks vs low-level tasks?  
    \item Can grading practices influence how students approach challenging tasks?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Reflection}
\textbf{Guiding questions for reflection:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Think of a recent student mistake you observed. Was it a slip, error, or misconception? How did you respond?  
    \item How does understanding slips vs errors change how we plan instruction or support students?  
    \item How else could grading strategies be adapted to encourage learning rather than just penalizing mistakes?
\end{itemize}
